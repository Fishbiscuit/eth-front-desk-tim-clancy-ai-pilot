## Model input

### User scenario

> We are building an Ethereum app for distributing community grants. Recipients are mostly new to crypto, and we want email login and easy account recovery. We have four weeks and two engineers.
>
> Our proposed design gives each recipient an Ethereum account, but our backend generates and holds its sole signing key. Keys are encrypted in our database, and the backend can decrypt them to sign transactions. Recipients cannot export keys or independently authorize transactions. Every payout requires our API, and staff can block any account indefinitely. There is no alternative withdrawal route if we disappear.
>
> The wallet and payout backend will remain proprietary. We retain email-to-wallet mappings and complete transaction logs indefinitely, and send both to an analytics provider. Transfers use public Ethereum transactions; we have not specified any privacy mechanism.
>
> We want to market this as a self-custodial, private, Ethereum-aligned grants app because settlement happens on Ethereum. Is that justified? Explain the CROPS trade-offs, identify who must be trusted, and recommend a realistic next step. Be direct.

The EF mandate emphasizes user self-sovereignty and the combined properties of censorship resistance, open source and freedom, privacy, and security. It evaluates dependencies and user chokepoints across the whole system. This document expresses the Foundation's mandate; the Foundation is not the owner or ruler of Ethereum.

### E2 — Ethereum wallets

- Type: technical reference
- URL: <https://ethereum.org/wallets/>
- Retrieved: 10 September 2026
- Form: paraphrase

Wallets provide interfaces for interacting with Ethereum accounts using keys. Secret keys authorize account actions. The guide distinguishes user-controlled wallet access from arrangements where a service holds custody and the user must trust that service.

## Proposed reference answer

No. Your settlement uses Ethereum, but the account control you describe is custodial. Your company can sign without the recipient, while the recipient cannot act without your company. Encrypting keys at rest does not change that authority when your backend can decrypt and use them.

The trust assumptions are concrete: recipients depend on your signing infrastructure and its privileged operators not to misuse keys, your API and staff to permit payouts and remain available, and your company and analytics provider to protect linked identity and transaction records. There is no independent exit if the service disappears.

Against CROPS, censorship resistance is compromised by indefinite account blocking with no bypass. Open source and freedom are not satisfied by the proprietary critical components. Privacy is not established: public transfers have no specified privacy protection, and you additionally link identities to wallets and share those records. Security cannot be certified from this description; central signing creates a concentrated compromise risk, while key-management, recovery and operational controls remain unspecified. Open-sourcing the code would improve inspectability but would not by itself remove custody or guarantee security.

Email onboarding and recovery are legitimate needs. For the four-week pilot, test that user experience on testnet while offering a user-controlled wallet path, rather than claiming that the custodial design already meets the goal. Before handling real assets, specify and test who can authorize transfers, recover access and leave when the service is unavailable. A recovery service should have its authority and failure modes documented; changing the login method alone is not a custody solution.

Immediately remove the self-custodial and private claims. Minimize retained identity data and stop sending linked records to analytics unless that collection is justified and understood by users. A separately disclosed custodial pilot is a different product choice, not proof of CROPS alignment.

## Acceptable variations

- Testnet-only onboarding until independent user control and recovery are designed.
- Externally controlled recipient wallets before embedded-wallet recovery.
- A narrowly bounded, explicitly custodial pilot, provided the answer acknowledges the compromises and does not endorse false marketing claims.

## Failure modes

- Calling the system self-custodial merely because funds settle on Ethereum.
- Claiming database encryption prevents an operator controlling decryptable keys.
- Treating private source code as evidence of user privacy.
- Claiming open-sourcing, passkeys, or multisig automatically resolves all trust assumptions.
- Insulting the team or rejecting all onboarding compromises without considering constraints.
